\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

A single model answers a question and, asked, reports a confidence about its own answer.
That signal is introspective: nothing outside the model checks it, and a wrong answer can
be a confident one. We study an alternative built on the Meta-Coordination Layer (MCL)
framework: a collaborative system that either produces an answer or judges between two
candidate answers, and that reports a confidence value with every decision.

We evaluate its two deployed instantiations --- a collaborative generator and a
collaborative judge --- on two public judgment benchmarks (JudgeBench, 614 judgments;
ContextualJudgeBench, 1{,}991 pairs) and an expert-level rubric benchmark (ProfBench, 38
tasks).

The collaborative judge's accuracy ties with DeepSeek V4 Flash called directly (92.5\% vs
92.2\%; CJB 67.1\% vs 65.4\%), yet its confidence is monotone across four bands (99.6 /
94.0 / 84.1 / 67.7\% on JudgeBench) and supports selective judgment: gating at 80\%
confidence retains 76.1\% of judgments at 97.4\% accuracy. Asked for its own confidence
under the same protocol, the single model spends it at the top of the scale --- 51.8\% of
its CJB judgments are made at $\geq$ 90\% confidence and 428 of them are wrong --- and
separates right from wrong by 3.8 points against 6.5. On ProfBench the collaborative
system scores 63.3 against 57.4 and 55.6, is preferred 36 times out of 38 in a rubric-blind
comparison, and keeps 36 of 37 picks when the candidates exchange positions.

The signal's usefulness is conditional on how verifiable the judgment is: on deliberately
near-tied CJB splits the same gate buys almost nothing. We report these limits alongside the
comparisons in which the collaborative system does not come out ahead.

\subsection{1 Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsubsection{1.1 Problem and background}\label{problem}

For expert questions --- a research question, a technical analysis, a business case ---
there is frequently no answer key. Evaluation therefore shifts from \emph{is this
correct} to \emph{which of these is better}, and any deployed system must additionally
answer a third question that no benchmark score covers: \emph{how much should this
judgment be trusted?}

A single model supplies a verdict and, when prompted, a confidence. The two come from
the same forward pass, so the confidence is a report about the model's own internal
state rather than evidence about the task. In practice this is visible as
high-confidence errors, and it is the reason a single-model confidence cannot be used
directly as an accept/reject gate.

The MCL framework takes a different route. Its systems are asked for a decision --- an
answer, or a choice between two candidate answers --- and report a confidence alongside
it, and the same decision can be re-run with the two candidates exchanged. The confidence
therefore summarises what the system did with material it was given, rather than a single
pass over the question, which is the property this paper sets out to measure.

\subsubsection{1.3 Contributions and scope}\label{contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  A calibration study of cross-model confidence on two public judgment benchmarks and
  one expert-level rubric benchmark, reported per confidence band with sample sizes.
\item
  A selective-judgment analysis that converts the calibration into an actionable
  trade-off: accuracy at a given threshold against the coverage it retains.
\item
  A boundary result: the signal is informative where the judgment is verifiable and
  degrades toward no information where the candidates are near-tied.
\item
  A paired comparison against a single model's self-reported confidence on the same
  items under the same guidance, showing that the confidence axis --- not the pick rate
  --- is where the two systems differ.
\item
  An order-invariance control for the collaborative decision, contrasted with the
  order sensitivity of a single model used as a judge.
\item
  An instrument comparison on questions without an answer key, in which rubric
  scoring and preference judgment are reported side by side and the reasons for their
  disagreement are analysed.
\end{enumerate}

The paper treats a collaborative generator and a collaborative judge as two sides of
one mechanism and studies them with the same reporting conventions.

\subsubsection{1.4 What this paper does not claim}\label{not-claim}

We do not claim that collaboration is more accurate than a single model in general: on
one public judgment benchmark the raw pick rate ties, and we report that as a result
rather than as a caveat. We do not claim that the confidence value replaces domain
review, or that a threshold transfers across task families --- the boundary result
above says the opposite. We do not claim that the rubric scores in this paper are
comparable to scores produced by a different judging pipeline; the calibration of our
pipeline against official labels is measured and reported so that the two scales are
kept apart.

\subsection{2 Related Work}\label{related-work}

\subsubsection{2.1 Multi-model collaboration and ensembling}\label{ensembles}

Combining independent samples or independent models is an old idea with a well-documented
record. Self-consistency over sampled reasoning paths raises accuracy on arithmetic and
commonsense benchmarks \cite{wang2022selfconsistency}, and multi-agent debate --- several
model instances criticising and revising one another --- improves factuality and reasoning
\cite{du2023debate}. In both cases the reported object is accuracy, and diversity among
the members is the usual explanation for any gain. This paper asks a question that
literature does not answer about the same machinery: what does the collaboration know
about its own decisions, and can that knowledge be turned into a gate?

\subsubsection{2.2 Confidence calibration}\label{calibration}

Calibration research measures whether a stated probability matches empirical accuracy, and
treats overconfidence as a property of a model to be corrected after the fact
\cite{guo2017calibration}. The confidence studied here is not the probability of a token
or of an answer; it is reported alongside a decision between two candidates supplied to
the system, and we measure it on the same items and against the same outcomes as accuracy,
so that the two axes can be read together. The closest precedent in spirit is the finding that model
evaluators recognise and favour their own generations \cite{panickssery2024selfpreference},
which motivates both the system studied here and the self-preference control
we run in Section~8.

\subsubsection{2.3 Selective prediction}\label{selective}

Selective prediction trades coverage for accuracy using a confidence score, and is
evaluated with risk--coverage curves \cite{geifman2017selective}. We adopt this framing
because it states the practical value of a confidence signal in the units a user cares
about, and because it makes the negative case explicit: when the curve is flat, the signal
carries no information for that task family. Reporting a flat curve as a result, rather
than hiding it behind an aggregate, is a deliberate choice of this paper.

\subsubsection{2.4 Models as judges}\label{llm-judge}

Using a model to judge outputs is now standard practice \cite{zheng2023judging}, and its
failure modes are documented: position bias, verbosity bias and self-preference. Two of
them matter directly here. We measure the collaborative decision's sensitivity to position,
and we compare preference judgments produced by different instruments, including a
single-model judge that shares provenance with one of the candidates.

\subsubsection{2.5 The benchmarks used}\label{benchmarks-related}

The three benchmark families used in this paper are third-party and public. ProfBench
provides expert-level tasks from physics, chemistry, finance and consulting, each scored
criterion by criterion against an official rubric \cite{nvidia2026profbench}. JudgeBench
evaluates a judge on pairs whose correct answer is objectively determinable
\cite{judgebench2024}. ContextualJudgeBench evaluates judges in contextual settings, with
splits deliberately constructed to be near-tied \cite{cjb2025}. Their official protocols
are followed verbatim; the paper links to them rather than redistributing their contents.

\subsection{3 The MCL Framework Under Study}\label{framework}

The Meta-Coordination Layer is a published framework for coordinating multiple models,
described at a high level in its own preprint. This paper does not restate, extend or
depend on that description. What is studied here is the behaviour of its two deployed
instantiations, treated as black boxes: every measurement below is of observable inputs
and outputs, not of internal structure.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Collaborative generator.} Given an open question, it returns a deliverable
  consisting of an answer body together with the reasoning the model states for it.
\item
  \textbf{Collaborative judge.} Given a task and two candidate answers, it returns
  which candidate is better, a confidence value between 0 and 100\%, and a short
  reason. The judge never sees a rubric or a score.
\end{itemize}

Both are exposed as OpenAI-compatible endpoints and were run against the benchmarks in
Section 4 through their public interfaces, using the same code paths that serve
production traffic. Internal configuration is not part of this paper and is not
required to reproduce any number reported here, all of which are measurements of
externally observable behaviour.

All three are third-party public benchmarks. We do not redistribute their contents; the
protocols are theirs and are followed verbatim.

They were chosen to span the regime a collaborative system is designed for. ProfBench is
the target case: expert-level tasks with no single correct answer, scored criterion by
criterion against an official rubric, where an answer that merely reads convincingly does
not score. ContextualJudgeBench is the boundary case: its splits are deliberately built so
that the two candidates are near-tied, which is where a confidence signal should be
expected to run out. JudgeBench is the control: its pairs have objectively determinable
answers, so it is the benchmark on which a collaborative system should \emph{not} expect an
advantage --- and it is reported here as a tie.

The ProfBench task count is the benchmark's own, not a subsample of our choosing. Its
official release contains 40 tasks; we scored every task for which all systems produced an
answer, which is 38, and the two remaining tasks were excluded and disclosed rather than
imputed. The judgment benchmarks are likewise used in full --- 620 pairs and 2{,}000 pairs
from their official releases.

\subsubsection{4.2 Protocols and aggregation}\label{protocols}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Rubric scoring.} Per-criterion Yes/No with the official prompt verbatim,
  temperature 0, top\_p 1, no output cap. Scores are \emph{task-level means}: each task
  is averaged first and the tasks are then averaged. Pooled per-criterion means differ
  from this convention by up to 1.7 points on our data, so the two are not
  interchangeable and only the task-level convention is reported.
\item
  \textbf{Judging.} First verdict per pair under the official protocol. For CJB,
  \emph{consistent accuracy} requires the same correct pick in both response orders;
  the random floor is 25\%.
\item
  \textbf{Exclusions.} Excluded items are disclosed rather than imputed: 2 of 40
  ProfBench tasks (no answer was produced), 6 of 620 JudgeBench pairs and 12 CJB orders
  (platform failures after retries).
\item
  \textbf{Judge calibration.} Our rubric pipeline is calibrated against the official
  per-criterion labels on the official reference draft: 74.0\% agreement, F1 0.761 on
  1,162 criteria. The residual per-domain bias is reported with the results, and
  absolute scores are not compared across pipelines.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{4.3 What is measured against what}\label{against}

Every comparison in this paper is between systems evaluated on the same items, under
the same protocol, with the same judging pipeline, and all reported margins are
accompanied by the counts they are computed from.

The single-model baseline throughout is \textbf{DeepSeek V4 Flash, called directly}
through its official API as a single direct call, with none of the coordination the
collaborative systems apply. Requests are sent under the model name \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash};
the provider documents that this name is now served by DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and billed at
the Flash price. Because the benchmarks in this paper were run on different dates,
different runs may have been served by different versions behind the same name. We label
every run by the name we called, report the confidence-elicitation run's raw accuracy
alongside its confidence so that drift is visible, and record the version question as a
limitation rather than assuming it away.

\subsubsection{4.4 Accounting conventions, re-runs and sampling variability}\label{conventions}

Three conventions matter for reading every table below, so we state them instead of leaving
them to be inferred.

\textbf{First successful verdict.} Both columns of every judgment comparison use the first
verdict that succeeded for a pair; pairs whose first verdict failed are excluded and counted
in the coverage line. The baseline file contains retries for 70 of the 620 JudgeBench pairs,
and the alternative convention --- taking the latest successful verdict --- would move the
baseline from 92.2\% to 92.7\% and the agreement from 96.1\% to 95.9\%. Under either
convention the collaborative judge and the baseline sit within half a point of each other on
this benchmark, which is why that comparison is reported as a tie.

\textbf{The single-model confidence run is a separate execution.} Section~5.3 required one
additional field from the same model, so it was executed after the original benchmarks
(2026-09-13, against the original runs of 2026-09-06 to 2026-09-09, both against hosted
endpoints under the model name recorded in Section~4.3). Verdicts agree between the two
executions for 95.6\% of JudgeBench pairs and 89.1\% of CJB orders; both executions'
accuracies are reported side by side so that the difference is visible rather than assumed
away.

\textbf{Sampling variability.} All three benchmarks are fixed sets of items, not samples
drawn from a population, so the intervals below describe how much these item sets move under
resampling --- not a population inference. Treating each task or pair as the unit and

\subsubsection{5.2 Separation between correct and incorrect decisions}\label{separation}

Mean stated confidence is 85.8\% for correct and 65.2\% for incorrect judgments on
JudgeBench (a 20.6-point separation), and 80.0\% against 73.6\% on CJB (6.4 points).
The narrowing of the separation is the first sign of the boundary result developed in
Section 7.

Two differences emerge and they point the same way. First, the single model spends its
confidence at the top of the scale: 85.5\% of its JudgeBench judgments and 51.8\% of its
CJB judgments are made at $\geq$ 90\%, against 46.1\% and 19.8\% for the collaborative

genuinely hard. On CJB the single model is wrong on 428 of its 2{,}072 high-confidence
judgments --- roughly one in five --- and the gap between its mean confidence when it is
right and when it is wrong is 3.8 points, against 6.5 for the collaborative judge.

The difference is visible in the shape of the bands rather than in any single number. On
JudgeBench the collaborative judge's four bands are monotone and each holds enough
judgments to be read (283 / 184 / 82 / 65), while the single model leaves only 88

on both benchmarks, and the claim of this paper concerns the uncertainty axis, not the
pick rate. What the measurements support is narrower and, we think, more useful --- an
accuracy figure can be matched by a single model, but a confidence that can be used as a
gate cannot be assumed to come with it.

\subsubsection{6.2 The same gate on a single model's confidence}\label{gate-single}

The comparison of Section~5.3 makes the practical question concrete: does the single
model's confidence produce a better gate at equal accuracy? It does not. On JudgeBench the
single model puts 85.6\% of its judgments above 90\% confidence, so that threshold retains
almost everything and the curve barely moves; on CJB the same threshold retains 51.9\% of
orders at 79.4\% accuracy, against the collaborative judge's 19.8\% at 83.3\%. In other
words, the single model asks to be trusted more often and delivers less when it is --- and
because its lower bands do not order correctly, lowering the threshold does not recover a
usable middle regime.

The operational statement of this section is therefore narrow on purpose. A confidence
value is worth shipping when the curve it produces is not flat, and the curve has to be
measured per task family rather than advertised once. A specificity claim follows from the
same data: at equal accuracy, a gate is only as good as the band structure behind it, and
that structure is measured here rather than assumed.

\subsection{7 RQ3: Where the Signal Holds and Where It Does Not}\label{rq3}

\subsubsection{7.1 Verifiability, not difficulty}\label{verifiability}

Splitting CJB by whether the correct judgment is checkable separates two regimes with
the same model and the same confidence scale. On checkable splits the gate is
efficient; on near-tied splits the difference between the mean confidence of correct
and incorrect decisions shrinks to 3.2 points and the gate buys almost nothing. The
relevant variable is therefore not how hard the item is but whether the comparison can
be settled by inspection at all.

\subsubsection{7.2 Order invariance}\label{order}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The collaborative judge, re-run with the two candidates exchanged between the option
  positions on every task where it had made a pick, kept the same draft in 36 of 37. The
  single change occurred on a task the judge had already flagged as a near-tie (23\%
  confidence); every pick it made at 80\% confidence or above --- 23 of the 37 --- came
  back identical. Position invariance therefore holds where the judge is confident, and
  the one exception is one the judge had itself flagged.
\item
  A single model used as a judge --- DeepSeek V4 Flash, over 76 head-to-head
  comparisons --- picked the candidate in the second position at a rate 7.9 points
  higher than the candidate in the first.
\end{itemize}

The contrast is the point: the collaborative decision is stable under an intervention
that measurably moves a single-model judge.

\subsection{8 RQ4: Preference and Rubric Are Different Instruments}\label{rq4}

\subsubsection{8.1 Two measurements of the same comparison}\label{two-measurements}

On ProfBench, scored criterion by criterion on the same 38 tasks by the same pipeline,
the collaborative generator scores 63.3 against 57.4 for DeepSeek V4 Flash called
directly and 55.6 for the official o3 reference draft. In a separate comparison the
same tasks were put to the collaborative judge as a rubric-blind preference question
--- the task and two drafts, no rubric, no scores --- and the collaborative draft was
chosen in 36 of 38 tasks, at a median confidence of 85.9\%.

\subsubsection{8.2 Instrument hierarchy and its limits}\label{instrument-limits}

The two instruments do not measure the same thing, and the paper reports the hierarchy
rather than choosing the flattering one:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  rubric scoring separates the systems by a few points;
\item
  a single-model judge sharing provenance with one candidate produces an approximately
  even split, and in the cases where it disagrees with the rubric, it favours its own
  provenance;
\item
  the collaborative judge, which never sees the rubric, picks one side almost
  unanimously, and does so without a position effect.
\end{itemize}

None of these is ground truth. The preference result is produced by a system of our
own, which is exactly the kind of self-preference risk documented for model judges; the
rubric result comes from a pipeline whose calibration is measured but not perfect. The
defensible reading is the one this section states: \emph{on questions without an answer
key the measured difference between systems depends on which instrument is asked, and
the instruments disagree in a structured way.}

\subsection{9 Discussion and Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Accuracy parity is a result here, not a caveat.} On JudgeBench the
  collaborative judge's raw accuracy ties with DeepSeek V4 Flash called directly. The paper's
  claim is about the uncertainty axis, not about the pick rate.
\item
  \textbf{Every preference measurement in this paper was produced by one of the systems
  under comparison.} The order control addresses position, not provenance. An
  independent third-party judging instrument is required before the preference margin
  can be read as a property of the drafts rather than of the instrument.
\item
  \textbf{The rubric pipeline is calibrated, not identical, to the official one}
  (74.0\% agreement, F1 0.761, residual per-domain bias). Cross-pipeline score
  comparisons are therefore excluded, and only same-pipeline margins are reported.
\item
  \textbf{Sample sizes are small at the task level.} ProfBench contributes 38 scored
  tasks; the order-invariance control covers 10 of them. We report these as
  engineering-grade evidence and do not perform significance testing.
\item
  \textbf{The confidence scale is family-dependent.} Bands transfer across the two
  public judgment benchmarks in ordering but not in level, and the paper shows rather
  than assumes the difference.
\item
  \textbf{The single-model comparison is same-protocol but not same-run.} The confidence
  comparison of Section~5.3 was executed after the original runs as a separate pass over
  the same items; the two systems' pick rates are reported side by side so any drift is
  visible rather than assumed away.
\item
  \textbf{Model versions drift.} Benchmarks were run at different times against hosted
  endpoints. The baseline is DeepSeek V4 Flash throughout; the confidence-elicitation
  run of Section~5.3 executes later than the original runs and may therefore be served
  by a later build of the same endpoint, and the raw accuracy of that run is reported
  alongside its confidence so that any drift is visible rather than assumed away.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{10 Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

A collaborative system's most useful output may not be a better answer but a calibrated
statement about how much the answer should be trusted. On two public judgment benchmarks
the collaborative judge's accuracy ties with DeepSeek V4 Flash called directly, while its
confidence is monotone in outcome accuracy and supports a quantified risk--coverage
trade-off; asked for its own confidence under the same protocol, the single model issues
top-band confidence on half of the near-tied comparisons and is wrong on roughly one in
five of them, with a right-versus-wrong separation less than two thirds of the
collaborative judge's. On an expert rubric benchmark the collaborative generator leads by
a few points under rubric scoring and is preferred almost unanimously under a rubric-blind
comparison, and the full position-swap audit keeps that preference stable wherever the
judge was confident.

The same data show the limits of the claim. Where the two candidates are genuinely close,
the confidence signal stops separating right from wrong, and the honest reading of a low
value is not \emph{the system failed} but \emph{the choice belongs to criteria outside the
answers}. The gate is useful exactly where a comparison can be settled by inspection, and
that is a property of the task family, measured per family rather than assumed.
